    Fundamental Distributions

    Univariate fundamental distributions¶

    This section contains univariate fundamental distributions in the sense that they cannot refer to any other distribution -- only to functions, parameters and exactly one variable.

    Argus distribution¶

    The Argus background distribution is defined as

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{ArgusPdf}(m, m_0, c, p) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \cdot m \cdot \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{m}{m_0} \right)^2 \right]^p \cdot \exp\left[ c \cdot \left(1 - \left(\frac{m}{m_0}\right)^2 \right) \right] \end{aligned} \]

    and describes the ARGUS background shape.

    • name: custom unique string
    • type: argus_dist
    • mass: name of the variable \(m\) used as mass
    • resonance: value or name of the parameter used as resonance \(m_0\)
    • slope: value or name of the parameter used as slope \(c\)
    • power: value or name of the parameter used as exponent \(p\).

    Chebychev distribution¶

    The Chebychev distribution is defined as a polynomial in Chebychev polynomials of the first kind:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{ChebychevPdf}(x, a_1,\ldots,a_n) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}}\left(T_0(z) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i T_i(z)\right), \quad z = 2\frac{x-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}} - 1 . \end{aligned} \]

    The coefficient of the constant term \(T_0(z)=1\) is implicit and fixed to one.

    • name: custom unique string
    • type: chebychev_dist
    • x: name of the variable \(x\)
    • coefficients: array of coefficients \(a_i\) for the non-constant Chebychev terms. The first coefficient belongs to \(T_1(z)\).

    Uniform distribution¶

    The continuous uniform distribution is defined as:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{UniformPdf}(x) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \end{aligned} \]
    • name: custom unique string
    • type: uniform_dist
    • x: name of the variable \(x\)

    Crystal Ball distribution¶

    The single-sided Crystal Ball line shape, composed of a Gaussian distribution at the core, connected with a power-law distribution describing one of the tails, given by

    \[ \text{CrystalBallPdf}(m;m_0,\sigma,\alpha,n) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \begin{cases} \exp \left( - \frac{1}{2} t^2 \right), & \mbox{for } t > -\left| \alpha \right| \\ A \cdot \left(B - t\right)^{-n}, & \mbox{for } t \leq -\left| \alpha \right| \end{cases} \]

    where \(t = \frac{m - m_0}{\sigma}\) if \(\alpha > 0\) and \(t = -\frac{m - m_0}{\sigma}\) if \(\alpha < 0\), and

    \[ \begin{aligned} A &= \left(\frac{n}{\left| \alpha \right|}\right)^{n} \cdot \exp\left(- \frac {\left| \alpha \right|^2}{2}\right) \\ B &= \frac{n}{\left| \alpha \right|} - \left| \alpha \right| \\ \end{aligned} \]

    For \(\alpha > 0\) the power-law tail is on the low side of \(m_0\), for \(\alpha < 0\) it is on the high side of \(m_0\).

    The keys are

    • name: custom string
    • type: crystalball_dist
    • m: name of the variable \(m\)
    • m0: name or value of the central value \(m_0\)
    • sigma: value or name of the width \(\sigma\)
    • alpha: value or name of \(\alpha\)
    • n: value or name of \(n\)

    The keys alpha_L, n_L, alpha_R, n_R, sigma_L and sigma_R must not be present. For a Crystal Ball distribution with two tails, see the double-sided Crystal Ball distribution below.

    Example: single-sided Crystal Ball distribution
    {
  "name": "cb",
  "type": "crystalball_dist",
  "m": "mass",
  "m0": "mass_peak",
  "sigma": "mass_width",
  "alpha": 1.5,
  "n": 3.0
}

    Double-sided Crystal Ball distribution¶

    The generalized Asymmetrical Double-Sided Crystal Ball line shape, composed of a Gaussian distribution at the core, connected with two power-law distributions describing the lower and upper tails, given by

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{CrystalBallPdf}(m;m_0,\sigma,\alpha_L,n_L,\alpha_R,n_R) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \begin{cases} A_L \cdot (B_L - \frac{m - m_0}{\sigma_L})^{-n_L}, & \mbox{for }\frac{m - m_0}{\sigma_L} < -\alpha_L \\ \exp \left( - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \left[ \frac{m - m_0}{\sigma_L} \right]^2 \right), & \mbox{for }\frac{m - m_0}{\sigma_L} \leq 0 \\ \exp \left( - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \left[ \frac{m - m_0}{\sigma_R} \right]^2 \right), & \mbox{for }\frac{m - m_0}{\sigma_R} \leq \alpha_R \\ A_R \cdot (B_R + \frac{m - m_0}{\sigma_R})^{-n_R}, & \mbox{otherwise}, \\ \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]

    where

    \[ \begin{aligned} A_i &= \left(\frac{n_i}{\left| \alpha_i \right|}\right)^{n_i} \cdot \exp\left(- \frac {\left| \alpha_i \right|^2}{2}\right) \\ B_i &= \frac{n_i}{\left| \alpha_i \right|} - \left| \alpha_i \right| \\ \end{aligned} \]

    The keys are

    • name: custom string
    • type: crystalball_doublesided_dist
    • m: name of the variable \(m\)
    • m0: name or value of the central value \(m_0\)
    • alpha_L, n_L: values or names of \(\alpha_L\) and \(n_L\)
    • alpha_R, n_R: values or names of \(\alpha_R\) and \(n_R\)
    • sigma: value or name of \(\sigma = \sigma_L = \sigma_R\). must not be used in conjunction with sigma_L or sigma_R.
    • sigma_L, sigma_R: values or names of \(\sigma_L\) and \(\sigma_R\). must not be used in conjunction with sigma.

    All four of alpha_L, n_L, alpha_R and n_R must be present. The width must be given either as sigma or as both sigma_L and sigma_R. The keys alpha and n must not be present. There is no shorthand for symmetric tails: a symmetric double-sided Crystal Ball distribution is described by giving the same value, or referencing the same parameter, for both sides.

    Example: asymmetric double-sided Crystal Ball distribution
    {
  "name": "cb_asym",
  "type": "crystalball_doublesided_dist",
  "m": "mass",
  "m0": "mass_peak",
  "sigma_L": "mass_width_L",
  "sigma_R": "mass_width_R",
  "alpha_L": 1.2,
  "n_L": 4.0,
  "alpha_R": 2.0,
  "n_R": 10.0
}
    Example: symmetric double-sided Crystal Ball distribution
    {
  "name": "cb_sym",
  "type": "crystalball_doublesided_dist",
  "m": "mass",
  "m0": "mass_peak",
  "sigma": "mass_width",
  "alpha_L": "cb_alpha",
  "n_L": "cb_n",
  "alpha_R": "cb_alpha",
  "n_R": "cb_n"
}

    Backward compatibility. Documents conforming to versions of this standard before 0.3 may contain distributions of type crystalball_dist with the keys alpha_L, n_L, alpha_R and n_R (or sigma_L and sigma_R). Implementations should read these as crystalball_doublesided_dist; this is unambiguous from the key names. Documents conforming to version 0.3 or later must use crystalball_doublesided_dist for the double-sided form. Note that crystalball_dist with alpha and n is now defined as the single-sided Crystal Ball distribution, in line with how existing documents were written in practice; earlier versions of this text read it as the symmetric double-sided form.

    Decay distribution¶

    The decay distribution describes a single- or double-sided exponential decay convolved with a resolution model \(R\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{DecayPdf}(t,\tau,R) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}}\,(B_{\text{type}} * R)(t), \end{aligned} \]

    where

    \[ \begin{aligned} B_{\text{type}}(t) = \begin{cases} \exp(-t/\tau), & \text{for SingleSided}, \\ \exp(t/\tau), & \text{for Flipped}, \\ \exp(-|t|/\tau), & \text{for DoubleSided}. \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
    • name: custom unique string
    • type: decay_dist
    • t: name of the decay-time variable \(t\)
    • tau: value or name of the decay constant \(\tau\)
    • resolutionModel: name of the resolution model \(R\)
    • decayType: integer selecting the decay basis, with 0 for SingleSided, 1 for DoubleSided, and 2 for Flipped.

    Exponential distribution¶

    The exponential distribution is defined as

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{ExponentialPdf}(x, c) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \cdot \exp(-c\cdot x) \end{aligned} \]
    • name: custom unique string
    • type: exponential_dist
    • x: name of the variable \(x\)
    • c: value or name of the parameter used as coefficient \(c\).

    Gaussian/normal distribution¶

    The Gaussian/normal distribution is defined as

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{GaussianPdf}(x, \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \end{aligned} \]
    • name: custom unique string
    • type: gaussian_dist or normal_dist
    • x: name of the variable \(x\)
    • mean: value or name of the parameter used as mean value \(\mu\)
    • sigma: value or name of the parameter encoding the standard deviation \(\sigma\).

    Generalized Normal distribution¶

    The generalized normal distribution is defined as

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{GeneralizedNormalPdf}(x, \mu, \alpha, \beta) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \exp\left\{ -\left( \frac{|x-\mu|}{\alpha} \right)^\beta \right\} \end{aligned} \]

    cf. the Wikipedia definition.

    This form is of particular use with values of \(\beta \sim 8\) to generate a flat-topped distribution useful for "2 point" parameter-constraints between models where there is no clear preference but their average should not be particularly favoured. (In such usage, the template points should ideally be located at \(|x| < \pm 1\), to avoid disfavouring a pure solution.)

    • name: custom unique string
    • type: generalized_normal_dist
    • x: name of the variable \(x\)
    • mean: value or name of the parameter used as mean value \(\mu\)
    • alpha: value or name of the \(\alpha\) parameter encoding the width; this corresponds to \(\sigma = \alpha/\sqrt{2}\) when \(\beta = 2\).
    • beta: power \(\beta\) to which the exponent is raised, \(\beta = 2\) corresponding to the \(\text{GaussianPdf}\) shape.

    Log-Normal distribution¶

    The log-normal distribution is defined as

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{LogNormalPdf}(x, \mu, \sigma) &= \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \frac{1}{ x } \exp\left( -\frac{(\ln(x)-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \end{aligned} \]
    • name: custom unique string
    • type: lognormal_dist
    • x: name of the variable \(x\)
    • mu: value or name of the parameter used as \(\mu\)
    • sigma: value or name of the parameter \(\sigma\) describing the shape

    Landau distribution¶

    The Landau distribution describes fluctuations in the energy loss of a charged particle traversing a thin layer of material. In terms of the conventional Landau density \(L\), it is

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{LandauPdf}(x,\mu,\sigma) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}}\,L(x;\mu,\sigma). \end{aligned} \]
    • name: custom unique string
    • type: landau_dist
    • x: name of the variable \(x\)
    • mean: value or name of the location parameter \(\mu\)
    • sigma: value or name of the scale parameter \(\sigma\)

    Poisson distribution¶

    The Poisson distribution of the integer variable \(x\) is defined as

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{PoissonPdf}(x, \lambda) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \frac{\lambda^x}{x!} \text{e}^{-\lambda}. \end{aligned} \]

    This is a discrete distribution: \(x\) must be a non-negative integer, and \(\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}\) is the sum of the expression over the integers in the current domain of \(x\). The behavior for non-integer values of \(x\) is undefined. A variable that can take non-integer values, such as the event count of an Asimov data set or the global observable of a constraint term, must instead be described by the continuous Poisson distribution (see Continuous Poisson distribution).

    • name: custom unique string
    • type: poisson_dist
    • x: name of the integer variable \(x\) (usually referred to as \(k\))
    • mean: value or name of the parameter used as mean \(\lambda\).

    Continuous Poisson distribution¶

    The continuous Poisson distribution of the real variable \(x\) is defined as

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{ContinuousPoissonPdf}(x, \lambda) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \frac{\lambda^x}{\Gamma(x+1)} \text{e}^{-\lambda} = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \exp\left(x \cdot \ln \lambda - \lambda - \ln \Gamma(x+1)\right), \end{aligned} \]

    where \(\Gamma\) denotes the Euler Gamma function.

    This is the continuation of the Poisson distribution (see Poisson distribution) to non-negative real values of \(x\). At integer values of \(x\) the two expressions coincide, but the two distributions differ in the admissible values of \(x\) and in their normalization: here \(\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}\) is the integral of the expression over the current domain of \(x\), which is in general not equal to the sum over the integers. Notably, the differences between the two might be significant for small values of \(x\) (below \(1\)). The continuous Poisson distribution is useful to deal with data sets with non-integer event counts, such as Asimov data sets [^asymptotics], and for constraint terms whose global observable is not an integer, such as the Barlow-Beeston-lite constraints of HistFactory (see HistFactory).

    • name: custom unique string
    • type: poisson_continuous_dist
    • x: name of the variable \(x\)
    • mean: value or name of the parameter used as mean \(\lambda\).

    Backward compatibility. Documents conforming to versions of this standard before 0.3 use the type poisson_dist for both the discrete and the continuous Poisson distribution. Such documents were in practice written by ROOT, which distinguishes the two by a boolean key integer that is not part of this standard: integer: false denotes the continuous form, integer: true the discrete form. Implementations reading documents conforming to versions before 0.3 should read a poisson_dist with integer: false as poisson_continuous_dist, and a poisson_dist with integer: true or without the key integer as the discrete distribution. Documents conforming to version 0.3 or later must use poisson_continuous_dist for the continuous form; in these documents, poisson_dist always denotes the discrete distribution and the key integer must not be present on either type.

    Polynomial distribution¶

    The polynomial distribution is defined as

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{PolynomialPdf}(x, a_0, a_1, a_2, ...) = \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \sum_{i=0}^n a_i x^i = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + ... \end{aligned} \]
    • name: custom unique string
    • type: polynomial_dist
    • x: name of the variable \(x\)
    • coefficients: array of coefficients \(a_i\). The length of this array implies the degree of the polynomial.

    Multivariate fundamental distributions¶

    This section contains multivariate fundamental distributions. They may refer to functions, parameters and more than one variable.

    Multivariate normal distribution¶

    The multivariate normal distribution is defined as

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{MvNormalPdf}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma) &= \frac{1}{\ensuremath{\mathcal{M}}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol\mu)^{\!\mathsf{T}} \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol\mu) \right), \end{aligned} \]

    with \(\boldsymbol\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\) being positive-definite.

    • name: custom unique string
    • type: multivariate_normal_dist
    • x: array of names of the variables \(\mathbf{x}\). This also includes mixed arrays of values and names.
    • mean: array of length \(k\) of values or names of the parameters used as mean values \(\boldsymbol\mu\)
    • covariances: an array comprised of \(k\) sub-arrays, each of which is also of length \(k\), designed to store values or names of the entries of the covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\). In general, the covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) must be symmetric and positive-definite, so that its inverse \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\) appearing in the density exists.

    Generic distribution¶

    Note: Users should prefer the specific distributions defined in this standard over generic distributions where possible, as implementations of these will typically be more optimized. Generic distributions should only be used if no equivalent specific distribution is defined.

    A generic distribution is defined by an expression that represents the PDF of the distribution in respect to the Lebesgue measure. The expression must be a valid HS3-expression string (see Section Generic Expressions).

    • name: custom string
    • type: generic_dist
    • expression: a string with a generic mathematical expression. Simple mathematical syntax common to most programming languages should be used here, such as x-2*y+z. The arguments x, y and z in this example must be parameters, functions or variables. The distribution is normalized by the implementation, so a normalization term should not be included in the expression. If the expression results in a negative value, the behavior is undefined.

    HistFactory distribution¶

    A HistFactory distribution is a joint probability distribution over bin counts \(n_i\), with independent Poisson probabilities whose expectations are supplied by a mixture of histogram templates. Its random variables are the bin counts, not the observable \(x\) used to define the fixed bins. The corresponding histfactory function (see Section Functions) provides the expected bin yields \(\lambda_i(\theta)\) from the sample mixture and its modifiers:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{HistFactoryPdf}(\mathbf{n}\mid\theta) &= \prod_i \text{PoissonPdf}(n_i, \lambda_i(\theta)) \end{aligned} \]

    This probability mass function is normalized over nonnegative integer count vectors \(\mathbf{n}\). The parameters \(\theta\) may include both parameters of interest and nuisance parameters.

    The prediction \(\lambda_i(\theta)\), the modifiers and their constraints, and an example are defined in Section HistFactory.

    • name: custom unique string
    • type: histfactory_dist
    • all further components as defined in Section HistFactory

    Relativistic Breit-Wigner distribution¶

    The relativistic Breit-Wigner distribution describes the line shape of a resonance in the mass spectrum of a two-particle system. The resonance couples to a non-empty list of two-body channels. The first channel specifies the observed final state.

    The variate \(m\) is the invariant (running) mass of the observed two-particle system. The parameter \(m_\text{BW}\) is the nominal mass of the resonance; it is not, in general, its pole mass. For \(m>m_{11}+m_{21}\), the density is

    \[ \begin{aligned} \label{eq:relativistic_breit_wigner_dist} \text{BreitWignerPDF}(m,m_\text{BW}) &=\frac{1}{\mathcal{M}}\frac{m\Gamma_1(m)} {\left|m_\text{BW}^2-m^2-i m_\text{BW}\Gamma(m)\right|^2},\\ \Gamma(m)&=\sum_i\Gamma_i(m). \end{aligned} \]

    The density is zero below the observed-channel threshold. Values at the threshold itself are defined by the limit from above, when finite; an integrable endpoint singularity does not change the probability distribution. \(\mathcal{M}\) normalizes the density over the current domain. The parameters and domain must give a finite, strictly positive normalization integral.

    The factor \(m\) in the numerator accounts for the Jacobian of the transformation from \(m^2\) to \(m\); its constant factor of two is absorbed in \(\mathcal{M}\). The factor \(\Gamma_1(m)\) includes the observed-channel phase space and angular-momentum dependence. This specifies a line-shape model without additional mass-dependent production factors or interference terms.

    • name: custom unique string
    • type: relativistic_breit_wigner_dist
    • mass: name of the observable mass variable \(m\)
    • resonance: value or name of the parameter \(m_\text{BW}\), the nominal mass
    • channels: non-empty array of channel structs, with the observed channel first

    The nominal mass must be strictly positive. For an open channel, the contribution is defined by

    \[ \begin{aligned} \Gamma_i(m)&=\Gamma_{\text{BW},i}\,n_{l_i i}^2(m)\rho_i(m),\\ \rho_i(m)&=\frac{2q_i(m)}{m},\\ q_i(m)&=\frac{\sqrt{\bigl(m^2-(m_{1i}+m_{2i})^2\bigr) \bigl(m^2-(m_{1i}-m_{2i})^2\bigr)}}{2m},\\ n_{l_i i}^2(m)&=z_i^{2l_i}(m)h_{l_i}^2(z_i(m)),\\ z_i(m)&=q_i(m)R_i. \end{aligned} \]

    Here \(q_i(m)\) is the real, non-negative breakup momentum. If the evaluation range includes closed channels, use the analytic continuation described in the note below.

    The Blatt-Weisskopf factors use the convention of Eqs. (50.33)-(50.34) in Ref. [^pdg2023]:

    \[ h_0^2(z)=1,\qquad h_1^2(z)=\frac{1}{1+z^2},\qquad h_2^2(z)=\frac{1}{9+3z^2+z^4}. \]

    For completeness, the same convention for any non-negative integer \(l\) is fixed by the polynomials

    \[ P_0(z)=1,\qquad P_1(z)=1-iz,\qquad P_{l+1}(z)=(2l+1)P_l(z)-z^2P_{l-1}(z)\quad(l\geq1),\qquad h_l^2(z)=\frac{1}{P_l(z)P_l(-z)}. \]

    Each channel struct contains:

    • name: name of the final state (optional)
    • Gamma: value or name of the non-negative width coefficient \(\Gamma_{\text{BW},i}\), with units of mass; it is not generally the partial width at the nominal mass
    • m1: value or name of the non-negative mass \(m_{1i}\) of the first daughter (optional, default \(0\))
    • m2: value or name of the non-negative mass \(m_{2i}\) of the second daughter (optional, default \(0\))
    • l: non-negative integer orbital angular momentum \(l_i\) (optional, default \(0\))
    • R: value or name of the strictly positive radius parameter \(R_i\), with units of inverse mass (optional, default \(3\,\mathrm{GeV}^{-1}\))

    The first channel's Gamma must be strictly positive. Masses and width coefficients must use a common mass unit, and radii must use the corresponding inverse unit so that \(q_iR_i\) is dimensionless. The default radius is converted accordingly when masses are expressed in a unit other than GeV.

    For a channel open at \(m_\text{BW}\), its partial width evaluated at the nominal mass is

    \[ \Gamma_i(m_\text{BW})=\Gamma_{\text{BW},i}\, n_{l_i i}^2(m_\text{BW})\rho_i(m_\text{BW}). \]

    The coefficient Gamma is therefore not generally equal to that partial width, even for \(l_i=0\). This definition retains the unnormalized phase-space and barrier factors; it does not use the nominal-partial-width convention of Eq. (50.35) in Ref. [^pdg2023]. No division by a phase-space factor at the nominal mass is made, so a channel may also have its threshold at or above \(m_\text{BW}\).

    A constant-width special case is one channel with \(m_1=m_2=0\) and \(l=0\). For \(m>0\), \(q=m/2\), \(\rho=1\), and \(n_0^2=h_0^2=1\), giving \(\Gamma(m)=\Gamma_{\text{BW},1}\) independently of \(R\).

    For example, a constant-width model with masses in GeV can be encoded as

    {
  "name": "resonance_shape",
  "type": "relativistic_breit_wigner_dist",
  "mass": "m",
  "resonance": 1.0,
  "channels": [
    {"Gamma": 0.1, "m1": 0.0, "m2": 0.0, "l": 0}
  ]
}

    Note on analytic continuation. A model with several channels may be evaluated in a mass range where the observed channel is open but some other channels are closed. Those channels still contribute to the denominator. When \(q_i^2(m)<0\), their breakup momentum is computed as \(q_i(m)=+i\sqrt{-q_i^2(m)}\), as in Eq. (50.36) of Ref. [^pdg2023], and their \(\Gamma_i(m)\) can consequently be complex. These continued contributions must be retained, rather than set to zero; they are not physical decay widths below threshold.

    The physical-sheet prescription, including the region below the pseudothreshold for unequal daughter masses, is

    \[ q_i(m)=\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+} \frac{\sqrt{m^2-(m_{1i}+m_{2i})^2+i\epsilon}\, \sqrt{m^2-(m_{1i}-m_{2i})^2+i\epsilon}}{2m}, \]

    with principal square roots for real \(m>0\). Thus \(q_i\) is positive imaginary between \(|m_{1i}-m_{2i}|\) and \(m_{1i}+m_{2i}\), and negative real below \(|m_{1i}-m_{2i}|\). Values at \(m=0\), if needed as a domain endpoint, are taken as limits from above. The rational barrier factors are continued as written: neither \(h_l^2(z)\) nor \(n_{l_i i}^2(m)\) denotes a modulus squared for complex arguments. Removable singularities of the complete line shape are evaluated by continuity. The observed-channel numerator is used only above its own threshold, where it is real and non-negative; the density remains zero below that threshold.
